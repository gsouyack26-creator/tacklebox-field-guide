Forage fish watch

What the industrial bunker fishery may mean for Northeast fishing

Evidence reviewed September 2026

Atlantic menhaden, commonly called bunker, feed striped bass, bluefish, weakfish, seabirds and marine mammals. The large reduction fishery lands menhaden at Omega Protein’s Reedville, Virginia plant for fish meal and oil. The evidence supports concern about forage pressure, especially where effort is concentrated, but it does not yet measure a specific loss of Northeast recreational catch caused by Omega.

What managers found

The stock passed its limits, but missed ecosystem targets

ASMFC’s 2025 assessment said Atlantic menhaden were not overfished and overfishing was not occurring under its ecological reference points. However, fishing mortality was above the ecosystem target and egg-producing capacity was below its target. Striped bass was the model’s most harvest-sensitive predator.

Management response

The 2026 coastwide quota was cut 20%

ASMFC reduced total allowable catch from 233,550 to 186,840 metric tons. Its projections still showed a 100% probability of fishing mortality remaining above the ecosystem target at that quota; a reduction of at least 50% would be needed to lower that probability. Managers chose a smaller one-year cut while weighing economic effects.

Local-pressure question

Coastwide health can hide concentrated effects

Virginia caps reduction harvest inside Chesapeake Bay at 51,000 metric tons. ASMFC opened a review of cutting that cap by as much as 50% and spreading effort through the season, citing concern that concentrated effort may affect other Bay fisheries and ecology. A coastwide assessment cannot by itself prove or rule out local depletion.

Predator evidence

Osprey results raise concern, with an active scientific dispute

A lower-Bay study found declining osprey reproduction, fish delivery and menhaden in the diet; a food-supplement experiment also improved nesting performance, supporting prey limitation. VIMS scientists later argued the study’s coastwide menhaden index was a weak proxy for fish available around local nests and did not establish harvest as the cause. Both findings matter.

Bottom line for anglers

Removing bunker reduces prey biomass available to the food web, and current ecosystem targets call for less fishing pressure. The strongest defensible claim is that industrial harvest adds forage pressure and creates a credible localized-depletion risk. Evidence is not strong enough to assign Omega a precise decline in striped-bass abundance, migration, or angler catch rates across New Jersey and the Northeast.
